## How the D-10 Visa Actually Works

If you're finishing a bachelor's, master's, or PhD program in Korea and don't have a job offer lined up yet, the D-10 job-seeking visa is the bridge that keeps you legally in the country while you interview, network, and wait for E-7 sponsorship to come through. It's issued for 6 months at a time, renewable up to a combined total of 2 years, and unlike D-2 it does not require enrollment — you're explicitly here to look for work or prepare a startup (D-10-2/D-10-3 for entrepreneurship tracks exist too, but this guide focuses on the standard job-seeking route). The catch is that Korea Immigration doesn't hand it out automatically: you need to clear a points threshold, and the paperwork window opens right as your D-2 status is expiring, so timing matters as much as eligibility.

## The Points System That Decides Your Eligibility

Immigration uses a 200-point scale under the "Point System for D-10 Status," and you generally need **60 points minimum** to qualify (some regional or priority cases accept lower, but 60 is the safe baseline to plan around). Points come from a mix of academic background, Korean language ability, income potential, age, and prior work/internship experience in Korea.

| Category | Max Points | How It's Scored |
|---|---|---|
| Education level | 35 | PhD (35), Master's (30), Bachelor's (20) |
| TOPIK score | 20 | TOPIK 6 (20), TOPIK 5 (15), TOPIK 4 (10), TOPIK 3 (5) |
| Age | 25 | Highest for ages 18–25, decreasing gradually to 0 past mid-40s |
| Annual income (prior job/internship) | 10–20 | Scaled against GNI (Gross National Income) benchmarks |
| Korean work experience | 10–35 | Internships, research assistantships, or part-time work under D-2 |
| Field of study relevance | 5–15 | Bonus if your major matches a shortage occupation (IT, engineering, R&D) |
| Global top university bonus | 5 | If your degree is from a QS/Times-ranked top university abroad or a designated Korean university |

Two things trip students up: first, TOPIK matters even for STEM majors who assumed English-taught programs meant they didn't need Korean — a TOPIK 4+ meaningfully boosts your score and your job prospects. Second, income points are based on documented prior earnings (a paid internship, a research stipend), so undocumented cash work won't count. If you're short on points, doing a semester of a university-affiliated internship before graduation, or taking the TOPIK exam one more time, is often the fastest way to close the gap.

## Required Documents — What to Actually Gather

Immigration offices in Korea are strict about document completeness, and a missing item can bounce your application by weeks. Here's the standard checklist for a first-time D-10 application:

| Document | Notes |
|---|---|
| Application form (Hi Korea or paper) | Download from the HiKorea portal or pick up at the office |
| Passport + Alien Registration Card (ARC) | Original + copy |
| Certificate of (Expected) Graduation | Issued by your university's academic affairs office |
| Academic transcript | Full transcript, sealed or with university stamp |
| Points self-assessment sheet | Immigration-provided form where you calculate your own score |
| Supporting evidence for points | TOPIK certificate, internship contracts, awards, GNI-linked income proof |
| Bank statement | Minimum balance requirement (commonly ₩30,000–₩50,000 equivalent per month of stay, check current notice) |
| Passport photo | 3.5cm x 4.5cm, white background |
| Application fee | ₩60,000, paid at the office or via revenue stamp |

Keep digital scans of everything — many regional immigration offices now accept HiKorea's online pre-application, but you'll still need originals for the in-person visit. If your graduation certificate isn't ready yet (common for February graduates applying in December), a certificate of expected graduation from your department office works as a substitute.

## Application Timeline and Where to File

You should start the process **within your D-2 grace period**, which is typically the 2 months after your official graduation date, before your student status visa expires. Filing late risks falling out of legal status, which complicates every future visa application in Korea.

1. **1–2 months before graduation** — Confirm your points total using the self-assessment worksheet; gather TOPIK and internship documentation now, since certificates take time to issue.
2. **At graduation** — Request your Certificate of Graduation (or expected graduation) from your university.
3. **Within the grace period** — Book an appointment via HiKorea (hikorea.go.kr) for your local immigration office. Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Busan offices routinely have multi-week waits for appointment slots, so book as early as HiKorea allows.
4. **At the appointment** — Submit documents, pay the ₩60,000 fee, and get fingerprinted if this is your first extended stay-status change.
5. **Processing** — Typically 1–3 weeks. You'll receive a new ARC reflecting D-10 status.
6. **Every 6 months** — Reapply for extension, resubmitting proof of active job search (interview records, job application logs, or language study receipts) up to the 2-year cap.

A practical tip: many universities' international student offices (국제처) run D-10 transition workshops in the month before graduation — check with your school's office before going it alone, since they often review your points sheet for free and catch errors before Immigration does.

## Life on D-10: Work Rights, Part-Time Limits, and What Counts as "Job Seeking"

D-10 status is not the same as a work visa — you cannot be employed directly under it. What you *can* do is:

- **Part-time work with a permit**: Apply for a separate work permit (시간제취업허가) to work limited hours, similar to D-2 rules, usually capped around 25–30 hours/week depending on your points tier and field.
- **Internships and training**: Many D-10 holders use this period for a structured internship that later converts into E-7 sponsorship — this is the most common and reliable path off D-10.
- **Job search documentation**: When renewing, Immigration expects evidence you're actively seeking work — resumes submitted, interview invitations, or participation in job fairs like the KOTRA-run Contact Korea events for foreign talent.
- **No unemployment safety net**: D-10 is self-funded; there's no government stipend, so budgeting for rent and living costs (Seoul averages ₩600,000–₩900,000/month for a studio, excluding utilities) for the full 6-month block is essential before you file.

The goal state most students aim for is E-7 (Specific Activity) sponsorship from an employer, which converts your D-10 into a proper work visa — at that point the 2-year D-10 clock stops mattering.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I switch to D-10 while still enrolled, before my actual graduation date?**
No — D-10 requires proof of graduation or expected graduation within a very short window; you can't apply as an active full-time D-2 student mid-semester.

**What happens if I don't hit 60 points?**
You can still apply in some cases with a lower threshold if a job offer is imminent, but the safer route is boosting your score first — retaking TOPIK or securing a short internship are the fastest levers.

**Does D-10 count toward permanent residency (F-5) or long-term visas later?**
Time on D-10 itself generally doesn't count toward F-2/F-5 residency requirements, but it's a normal, expected stepping stone toward E-7, which does count once you're employed.

**Can I travel outside Korea on D-10?**
Yes, D-10 allows re-entry, but always confirm your re-entry permit status (many ARC holders now have multiple re-entry by default) before international travel near your renewal date.

**Is there a fee difference for extensions versus first-time applications?**
Extensions are typically the same ₩60,000 fee — budget for it every 6 months if you renew the full two years.

Plan your D-10 filing the same way you planned your D-2 renewal each semester: start early, keep every certificate the moment you earn it, and treat the points worksheet as a living document you update the day you pass TOPIK or finish an internship, not something you assemble the week before your appointment.
