## Where Japanese Graduates Actually Land Jobs in Korea

Every year, a growing number of Japanese students who complete a bachelor's or master's degree at a Korean university try to stay on and work in Seoul, Busan, or Incheon rather than returning home. The good news: Korea's tech, gaming, cosmetics, and manufacturing sectors actively recruit foreign talent who speak Japanese, since Japan remains one of Korea's largest export and tourism markets. The challenge: the hiring calendar, visa rules, and application culture are all distinctly Korean and nothing like Japan's *shūkatsu* system. This guide walks through exactly how to search, apply, and convert your student visa into a working one.

## Why Japanese Bilinguals Have an Edge

Companies value Japanese-Korean bilinguals for roles in customer support, localization, cross-border e-commerce, and Japan-market sales. Firms like Coupang, Naver, Kakao, Amorepacific, and gaming studios such as Krafton and NCsoft regularly post positions explicitly requiring "일본어 가능자" (Japanese-capable applicants). If you also speak English, you become a strong candidate for global business teams. TOPIK Level 5 or 6 is the realistic minimum for corporate roles, since interviews and internal communication are conducted in Korean; JLPT is rarely checked since employers assume native fluency.

## The Korean Hiring Calendar: Two Seasons, Not One

Unlike Japan's single-year *shūkatsu* cycle, Korean conglomerates (the *chaebol* — Samsung, LG, SK, Hyundai, Lotte) run **two structured hiring seasons** per year, called *gongchae* (공채). Smaller companies and startups hire year-round (*suksichaeyong*, 수시채용) as positions open up.

| Season | Application Window | Typical Start Date |
|---|---|---|
| Spring/Upper-half (상반기) | February–March | July |
| Fall/Lower-half (하반기) | August–September | January |
| Year-round (수시채용) | Ongoing | Varies by company |

Missing a *gongchae* window at a large firm often means waiting six months for the next one, so mark these dates as early as your third year of university.

## Where to Search: Portals That Actually Matter

Generic global job boards rarely surface Korean corporate postings. Use Korea-specific platforms instead:

- **Saramin (사람인)** and **JobKorea (잡코리아)** — the two largest general portals, covering everything from startups to conglomerates.
- **Work24 (워크넷)**, run by the Ministry of Employment and Labor, lists positions eligible for visa sponsorship and includes a foreigner-specific job section.
- **Wanted (원티드)** — popular with startups and IT companies, cleaner UI, good for tech/design/marketing roles.
- **LinkedIn Korea** — increasingly used by multinational and larger domestic firms for mid-to-senior roles.
- **University career centers** — most Korean universities have a *Career Development Center* offering resume clinics, mock interviews, and direct links to alumni recruiters; some hold job fairs targeting international graduates specifically.

## From D-2 to D-10 to E-7: The Visa Pathway

This is the part most guides gloss over, but it determines whether you can legally work at all. As a student, you hold a **D-2 (degree-seeking) visa**. After graduation, you cannot simply start working — you need to convert your status.

1. **D-10 (Job-Seeking Visa):** Apply for this immediately after graduation if you haven't secured a job offer yet. It grants up to 6 months (renewable up to 2 years total) to search for employment while staying legally in Korea.
2. **E-7 (Specific Activity Visa):** Once you have a signed job offer, your employer sponsors your switch to E-7, the standard work visa for foreign professionals. Processing typically takes 2–4 weeks through immigration.
3. **Point-based E-7-1 review:** Immigration evaluates salary level, your degree field, and company size against a points system — graduates of Korean universities and TOPIK holders receive bonus points, which meaningfully improves approval odds versus overseas applicants.

Some universities also help top graduates apply for the **D-8-4 (entrepreneurship)** or **F-2-7 (points-based long-term residency)** visas if you plan to start a business or have strong academic/work credentials, but E-7 remains the standard route for full-time employment.

## Building a Resume Korean Recruiters Will Actually Read

Korean resumes (이력서) and cover letters (자기소개서, often called "*jaso-seo*") follow a distinct format that differs from both Japanese *rirekisho* and Western resumes.

| Document | Korean Norm | Key Difference from Japan |
|---|---|---|
| 이력서 (Resume) | 1–2 pages, includes photo, TOPIK score, military status field (leave blank as foreigner) | Photo style less rigid than Japan's formal ID photo |
| 자기소개서 (Cover Letter) | 4–6 structured questions (motivation, strengths, conflict-resolution story) | Focuses on narrative storytelling, not just qualifications |
| Portfolio (for design/dev/marketing) | Often required even for non-creative roles | Rarely requested in traditional Japanese hiring |

Have a Korean-fluent friend, professor, or your university's career center review your *jaso-seo* — grammar and tone matter as much as content, and machine-translated Japanese-to-Korean text is easy for recruiters to spot.

## Interview Rounds: What to Expect

Most companies run 2–3 rounds: an initial HR/culture-fit interview, a job-specific or technical interview, and sometimes a final interview with executives. Group interviews (multiple candidates answering the same question in turn) are common at large firms. Expect direct questions about your visa status and long-term plans to stay in Korea — employers want reassurance you won't need to leave shortly after onboarding, so be ready to explain your E-7 eligibility clearly and confidently.

## Realistic Salary and Cost-of-Living Expectations

Entry-level salaries for foreign new graduates in Seoul typically range from ₩30–42 million per year (roughly ¥3.3–4.6 million) depending on industry, with tech and finance paying toward the higher end. Monthly take-home after tax and the mandatory 4-major insurances (health, pension, employment, industrial accident) is usually 85–90% of gross. Budget around ₩700,000–1,000,000/month for a studio (*wonroom*) in Seoul, plus the standard *jeonse* or *wolse* deposit system — ask HR whether your company offers a housing allowance or dormitory support, as many larger firms do for new international hires.

## Practical Timeline to Start Now

If you're graduating within a year, work backward from these milestones: secure TOPIK 5+ at least a year before graduation, attend your university's career fair in your final spring semester, apply to the *gongchae* window matching your graduation date, and file your D-10 application the moment your D-2 status is set to expire. Building a LinkedIn profile and a Korean-language portfolio during your final year — not after graduation — is what separates candidates who land offers within the D-10 window from those who run out of time.
