## What Makes a Korean Resume Different From a Western CV

If you've built a resume before, forget the format. A Korean **이력서 (ireokseo)** is a rigid, standardized document — usually a fillable table with fixed fields for photo, birthdate, and even family details on older templates — paired with a separate **자기소개서 (jasoseo)**, or self-introduction letter, that does the actual persuading. Employers in Korea (and HR staff reviewing international applicants) expect both documents together, in Korean, unless the job posting explicitly says English is fine. Tech startups and multinational offices (Coupang, Toss, foreign trading companies) are more flexible, but conglomerates (Samsung, LG, Hyundai) and most SMEs still expect the traditional two-document set. Getting this format right signals that you understand Korean workplace culture — often more important to reviewers than the content itself.

## Building the Ireokseo (이력서): Field by Field

Most companies now use their own online application forms, but the structure below is what nearly every template — paper or digital — follows. Photos matter more in Korea than in the US or much of Europe: use a professional headshot (증명사진), taken within the last six months, similar to a passport photo but with formal attire.

| Section | What to Include | Notes for International Applicants |
|---|---|---|
| 인적사항 (Personal Info) | Name (Korean + English), photo, birthdate, phone, email, address | Include your visa status/type if applying as a foreigner (e.g., D-10, F-2-7) |
| 학력사항 (Education) | Most recent degree first, school name, major, GPA if strong | List Korean-language institution names if you studied in Korea |
| 경력사항 (Work Experience) | Company, role, dates, key duties | Internships count — list them, even short ones |
| 자격증/어학 (Certifications/Language) | TOPIK level, licenses, IT certifications | TOPIK 4+ is often the unofficial minimum for office jobs; TOPIK 6 for client-facing roles |
| 기타 (Others) | Military service status (for Korean nationals), awards, activities | Foreign applicants can skip military status entirely |

Keep the entire document to one page unless the company explicitly asks for more detail. Dates follow the format YYYY.MM.DD, and Korean convention lists the most recent entry first for experience but sometimes oldest-first for education — check the template you're given, since both conventions exist.

## Writing the Jasoseo (자기소개서): The Real Differentiator

While the ireokseo is a checklist, the jasoseo is a structured essay, typically 500–1,000 characters per question (Korean employers count characters, not words). This is where hiring managers actually decide whether to interview you. Most companies provide 3–5 fixed prompts rather than a blank "tell us about yourself." The most common ones are:

1. **성장과정 (Growth Background)** — Your personal history and the values that shaped you. Avoid a childhood autobiography; connect it directly to a trait relevant to the job.
2. **지원동기 (Motivation for Applying)** — Why this specific company, not just "why this industry." Reference the company's products, recent news, or mission statement.
3. **입사 후 포부 (Career Goals After Joining)** — A 1–3 year plan showing you've thought about growth within the company, not just getting hired.
4. **직무 역량 (Job-Related Competency)** — Concrete evidence (STAR format: Situation, Task, Action, Result) that you can do the job.
5. **강점과 약점 (Strengths and Weaknesses)** — Koreans expect genuine self-reflection here, not a disguised strength like "I work too hard." Naming a real weakness with an improvement plan reads as more mature.

Write in **formal, humble tone** (겸손한 어조) — avoid overly boastful language. Korean corporate culture values team contribution over individual heroics, so phrase achievements as "우리 팀은 ~을 달성했습니다" (our team achieved) rather than centering yourself exclusively.

## Formatting, Fonts, and Submission Etiquette

Small formatting choices carry more weight in Korean hiring than in most Western contexts.

- **Fonts:** Use clean, professional Korean fonts — 맑은 고딕 (Malgun Gothic) or 나눔고딕 (Nanum Gothic). Avoid decorative fonts entirely.
- **File naming:** Save your file as `[Company]_[Position]_[YourName]_이력서.pdf`, not "resume_final_v3.pdf." HR staff scan hundreds of files, and a properly named file signals professionalism.
- **File format:** Submit as PDF unless the company's application portal requires a Word doc or has its own online form (many large companies use internal ATS systems where you type directly into web fields).
- **Character limits:** Online systems often enforce hard character counts per jasoseo question — write concisely and revise down, since overflow text gets cut off rather than flagged.
- **Proofreading:** A single typo or grammar mistake in the jasoseo is often treated as a red flag for attention to detail. If Korean isn't your first language, have a native speaker or a Korean language exchange partner review the final draft before submission.

## Where International Students Commonly Lose Points

Having TOPIK certification and strong grades isn't enough if the application itself doesn't follow local conventions. Based on common feedback from campus career centers and international hiring teams, these are the recurring issues:

| Common Mistake | Why It Hurts You | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Submitting an English-only resume to a Korean-language posting | Signals you haven't researched local norms | Provide a Korean-language version even if imperfect, or ask a language partner for help |
| Vague motivation section ("I want to work in Korea") | Sounds generic, not company-specific | Cite actual products, values, or recent news from the company |
| Listing TOPIK score without context | Reviewers can't judge practical fluency | Add a short note on how you use Korean (e.g., "conducted client calls in Korean during internship") |
| Overly long jasoseo answers | Ignoring character limits looks careless | Trim ruthlessly to the stated limit |
| No mention of visa eligibility | HR may assume sponsorship complications | State your current visa status and post-graduation eligibility (e.g., D-10 job-seeking visa, eligibility for E-7 sponsorship) |

If you're a graduating international student, note that many companies specifically ask about E-7 (Specific Activity) visa sponsorship in interviews, even if not on the written application. Having a one-line explanation ready — your current status and what changes upon hiring — prevents this from becoming an awkward surprise mid-process.

## Practical Timeline for Job-Seeking Season

Korean corporate hiring runs on two major cycles: **공채 (gongchae)**, the large-scale spring (March–April) and fall (September–October) recruitment drives used by conglomerates, and **수시채용 (susi chaeyong)**, rolling hire-as-needed postings common at startups and foreign firms. If you're targeting a conglomerate, prepare your ireokseo and jasoseo drafts at least a month before the application window opens, since these companies often require multiple jasoseo essays plus aptitude tests (인적성검사) before interviews. Startups move faster — sometimes interviewing within a week of application — so keep a polished, reusable draft ready rather than starting from scratch each time. University career centers (취업지원센터) at most Korean universities offer free jasoseo review sessions for enrolled international students; using this service before your first real application is one of the highest-value steps you can take.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a Korean name to apply?**
No — use your legal name as it appears on your passport and ARC (Alien Registration Card). Include the English spelling exactly as on official documents to avoid visa-related mismatches later.

**Is TOPIK required for all jobs?**
Not legally, but most Korean-language workplaces treat TOPIK 4 as a practical minimum and TOPIK 5–6 as competitive for client-facing or management-track roles. Tech roles at English-operating companies may not require it at all.

**Can I apply in English if I'm not confident in Korean yet?**
Only if the job posting is in English or the company explicitly accepts English applications (common at multinational offices and some startups). For Korean-language postings, submitting in English alone is usually a disqualifying signal.

**How long should my jasoseo be overall?**
Follow the platform's stated character limits exactly — typically 500–1,000 characters per question, 3–5 questions total. There is rarely a "one universal length"; it's set per company.
